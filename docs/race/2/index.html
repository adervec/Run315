Sodium

60-90 g an hour, and above 60 g it has to be a glucose-and-fructose mix, roughly 2:1. Glucose transport saturates near 60 g/h, so taking more of a single-sugar gel mostly leaves it in your stomach.

Drink to thirst inside this range rather than to a schedule. Overdrinking dilutes your blood sodium, which is more dangerous than mild dehydration and does put runners in hospital every year.

Spaced evenly rather than crammed late: the gut empties at a rate, and a backlog does not clear once you are working hard. Nothing in the last ten minutes, because it would not be absorbed in time to matter.

For a race this long, 36-48 hours of high-carbohydrate eating beforehand measurably helps. It is about eating more carbohydrate, not more food -- and the training taper is what makes room for it. Aim for 8–12 g of carbohydrate per kilo of body weight per day, for the two days before.

These are the published consensus ranges (Jeukendrup 2014; the joint position stand of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and the ACSM, 2016), not personal advice. Gut tolerance is individual and 90 g/h is a trained capacity rather than a starting point — practise it in training, never first on race day.
